Rank, rites and bonds

Five places in a pack, one mark that cannot be faked, and a bond made with a bite at the base of the neck.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Anu Canon Reference (v3). Tags mark anything less settled.

Three blood markers, five positions

Three markers are inherited: Alpha, Beta and Omega. Five positions exist in a pack: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Luna and Omega. Gamma and Luna are positions, not bloodlines, and are open to any blood, though usually of Alpha blood.

PositionNokRole
Alpha’ÁfáPack leader.
BetaBimáSecond in command.
GammaGaimáEnforcement and warrior chief.
Luna’AifáThe spiritual heart of the pack. Often Miyema-bonded to the Alpha, but not required.
OmegaÓmáThe base of the hierarchy.

The original Anu blessed by the deities were all Alpha. Later human recruits became Beta, and Omegas descend from Beta lines with humans. An Omega-blooded Anu who rises to the Alpha position is so rare that it reshapes a pack’s history.

The Rite of the Dreamthread

Nok ’ämbózisóróka, “Welcome of the Wolf.” It is a one-week solo wilderness survival and hunt held at a full moon, guided by the land and the youth’s spirit form, as a partner and not a tool. It is traditionally held before or during Tịkhètùbẹsé, the Great Shifting, a week-long holiday of heightened spirit connection. The youth is meant to meet their animal spirit and show it on return. Some packs name an initiate for a deed or for the spirit. Initiation Day is Rï’zháma.

On return the pack welcomes the initiate at a fire, chanting the children’s motto.

The düfari

Three upright paw glyphs joined by a horizontal line, the Dream Thread. It appears by magic, often at night, in black. An elder’s mark may fade to silver or umber. The mark is identical for every Anu, is not a tattoo and cannot be faked. Decided

  • Never on the neck, which is sacred to Miyema.
  • Common places: upper back, over the heart, forearm (warriors), shoulder blade, sternum or ribs (ritualists).
  • It tingles near other Anu, near Dreamgates and during high rituals.
  • It gives no combat bonus, but spirits, dreamwalkers and some Sekh recognise it.
  • Woven or stitched cloth versions may be worn at ceremonies.

Newborn alignment

A baby is placed on the Daimukómbusubí mat under Dreamleaf (Daigimí) smoke to find alignment: Shùnẹ, the Feather, or N’zaikís, the Fang. The Anu prefer the Zídifézí mat, a double-threaded braid. Adults who never received an alignment undergo Kihódání’obuwásóka. Birth month fixes the Dream-Tethered Sign. Alignment can shift with trauma or awakening.

Mates: Miyema

Thómítámün sees what each being needs and marks them so they may come together, or not. Háshùmün blesses and warms the bond. Pairing is always optional. Decided

  • The marking. Mates bite the base of the neck where it meets the collarbone, open their minds and promise themselves. The magic forms an upward crescent moon with a star and a dot above it, miyema bonyi, “together bond.” The vow is “We run as one.”
  • The effects. Deeper telepathy and shared emotion up to 50 miles. Sensation can be shared. Mates feel each other’s danger at any distance. A cross-species mate can receive the bond if love develops naturally.

Thómítámün uses any and all pronouns. Decided

The thread tradition

Every adult wears a thread in the hair, around the neck, or at the wrist or ankle. A loose thread means unmated. A shared or woven thread means bonded. A frayed or snapped thread means death, betrayal or mourning. The Nok name, Dúmùkwò, may still change. The wider tradition is on the thread tradition page.

MomentPhrase
Courting, braiding in a piece of the other’s thread“I carry your thread beside mine.”
Mate bond exchange“Your hunt is my hunt. Your thread is my thread.”
Mourning, a broken thread tied to the wrist“Your thread runs in the Dream. I follow the trail behind you.”
Burying the thread at the spring“I release your thread to the Hunt.”
Brotherhood or deep loyalty“Two threads. One hunt.”
Severance, in public at the fire“Your thread is cut. Your name falls into silence.”
A private break“Your thread frays. I walk without you.”

A severed thread is shameful for the one cut off, not for the one who cuts. Severance may be kept in clan memory songs as a warning.

Moral teaching

The Anu hold the thread tightly. Five layers of law run from two foundations to thirty threads, taught in phases from toddlerhood to age fifteen. The full text of each layer, in the Anu edition, is on the moral framework page.

  • Each day, morning and night, the devout recite five threads. Five a day over a six-day Nok week covers all thirty.
  • To pass initiation, the youth recites all thirty in order, explains twenty chosen at random, lives a test week judged by elder watchers, and passes a soul-mirror trial before a ’Anpésùmün priest.
  • Repeated unrepented breaks weaken, decay or turn the spirit. An Anu who dies with too many unrepented breaks has no Shadow Walker path. The spirit wanders and festers.
