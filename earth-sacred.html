Sacred arts and symbols

Makésho is the craft of the sacred. It is earned through devotion, not inherited, so a lifelong human devotee can outdo an untrained Sekh.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Earth reference documents. Tags mark anything less settled. Nok spellings follow the current reference documents.

Sacred plants

PlantWhat it is like
Daigímí, DreamleafA rare silvery-green plant that grows near ruins, death-touched places, thin-veil sites and temples blessed by Násítámün. White-lilac flowers glow during Daimùzónobu moon phases. It burns with the scent of wet stone, sage and cedarwood, with a metallic note the Nok call “memory rain.” Used to sharpen spiritual sensitivity.
Fùmakégimé, the Memory-Mark HerbA low shrub with deep blue-black berries that grows near places of long habitation, as though it remembers where people have lived. Tied to Wòtómün. It is not psychoactive. Its sacred property is permanence: marks made with it last.

Symbols

SymbolMeaning
Jaibüng’éh‘Èn’zíngámün’s sigil: a circle of flames with three tongues at the crown and one lower on the right. It is asymmetrical on purpose, because fire never burns evenly. It belongs to the goddess alone. An older temple form has thin, claw-like flames reaching out from a teardrop base. Both are sacred.
Jaibütùw’éhThe symbol of the faithful: the flame circle with an eye at its centre. “I am seen. I am of the flame.” Sekhs and human faithful wear it alike.
KhùnaimímakéThe moon-star mark that emerges on a Sekh at Rebirth. Khùm is moon, naimí is star, maké is mark. The formal name is Khùnaimí makéní.
Miyema bonyiThe Anu’s together-bond mark: a crescent moon with a star and a dot above it, blessed by Háshùmün.

Orders and clergy

  • The Order of Shadows (Shùkízòròmùna, the ShadowKeepers) is the human order tied to the Anu.
  • The Red Choir (Ikhùkímùna, also the Flame Followers or House of Ember) is the human clergy of the Flame Queen, who perform chant and blood rite.
  • The House of Ash and its Ghosthands tend threads that failed Rebirth.

Rites for the young

  • Dunésí‘ibo, the Twin-Thread ritual. A child is placed on the Daimùkómbusubí mat, where Dreamleaf smoke drifts across their face and elders chant to Násítámün, Time or Nyámün. Observers stay silent while the Dream unfolds. The outcome is read as Feather (Shùnẹ) or Fang (N’zaikís). The weaves Píjai and Zídifézí are used in it.
  • Kihódání‘obuwásóka, the awakening. A deeper rite that confirms a person’s true alignment when a quiz or earlier ritual is in doubt.
  • Dúmùkwò, the thread tradition. See The thread tradition.

Dreamleaf in daily life

Outsiders see herbal incense and quiet temples. Inside, the same smoke is a doorway. Dreamleaf is the reason Nẹránamün temples keep gardens.
