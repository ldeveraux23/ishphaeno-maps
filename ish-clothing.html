Clothing and Regalia

Ishphaeno dresses by draping, not tailoring. Rank is worn, and anyone at court can read it at a glance.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Ishphaeno garments and material culture reference. Materials are explained on Materials and Craft.

Draping philosophy

The court aesthetic is not medieval Europe with demons. It blends West African, Indian, Japanese and Greco-Roman drapery, filtered through an immortal civilisation. Tailoring exists, but draping is the highest art.

  • The silhouette. The basic wardrobe is long wrapped tunics, layered robes, split skirts, wrapped trousers, sashes, shoulder drapes, sleeveless outer coats and shawls. Everything moves, and even soldiers wear garments that billow when they walk.
  • No gendered clothing. Dress divides by profession, climate, rank and personality. A broad-shouldered blacksmith and a dancer may wear nearly the same base garment, differing in fabric, chest wrapping, sleeve length, jewellery, colour and hairstyle.
  • Chest manes. Heavy collars are avoided. The three eldest princes have ascot-like chest manes, so necklines are built to frame them with open V-necks, deep wrap fronts, asymmetric robes and crossed lapels.
  • Jewellery. Status sits at the face and limbs, not the throat: circlets, forehead chains, ear cuffs, multiple earrings, arm cuffs, stacked bracelets, anklets and elaborate belts.
  • Footwear. Not boots by default. Wrapped sandals, leather slippers, soft boots, split-toe shoes and high platform ceremonial shoes. Ports run barefoot surprisingly often. Hooved and draconic-legged princes wear different footwear from their humanoid-footed brothers.
  • Capes become shoulder drapes. The diagonal drape crosses the body from one shoulder to the opposite hip, and it is the form that clears a working wing pair. The vertical drop is a shorter length falling straight down one side. It is open to any prince without functional flight wings.

The rank wraps

Rank is worn through a single-cloth wrapping technique that scales in pleat count and difficulty. The garment never changes: one continuous length of cloth, wrapped and pleated, never cut into separate pieces. What changes is how many pleats the wearer can execute and how fine a cloth they can do it in.

TeMize through Veshael is pure technique. A single pass takes 3 to 4 yards, and a Veshael’s six pleats take up to 6 or 7 yards, each pleat about four fingers wide, gathered and tucked left of centre in the same method as a Nivi-style drape, then carried over one shoulder as a trailing panel. Heavier, finer fabric is harder to pleat evenly, not easier, so a Veshael’s Zenthiq is years of craft.

Lamqavael, the Prince’s Wrap

The same technique as Qethravael, one full skill tier higher. Eight pleats, each held taut and precise, and the count itself carries meaning: eight pleats for eight princes. It is cut from full Luthaevel, so the cloth shifts with the Toluqavel overhead before anything else goes on. A sash at the waist and a shoulder drape are standard daily dress with it. Neither is counted. The outer coat, the circlet and footwear are separate ceremonial and regalia categories. A prince in the wrap, sash and drape alone is not underdressed, and the outer coat works more like a smoking jacket than a base garment.

Xashvael, the Jamija Wrap

The mechanic changes at this tier. Twelve to fourteen passes of cloth are pulled taut so the fabric compresses around the body instead of falling open in pleats, closer to gift-wrap tension than to a sari. It is closed by small sovoq pins, one at each pass, and one load-bearing key pin sits at the collarbone or breastbone. Pull that pin and every pass sealed after it comes free at once. Each of the four Jamija handles the key pin differently: worn openly among a cascade of chainwork, hidden among a matched pair of decorative points, kept modular with extra release points at shoulder, hip and forearm, or replaced by a small engineered interlocking mechanism.

The wider Jamira nobility wear a deliberately looser version of the same silhouette, with slack pleats and gaps where skin shows by design, and an ordinary clasp instead of a key pin. The Jamija’s tightness says I am sealed and you have not earned the seam. A lesser succubus’s looseness says I choose what you see. Both are control, in opposite directions.

Regalia

Raqelath, the royal ring

The ring is named for Raqal, heart-stone, which is also a real gemstone of the same family as Xathqal and Sobraeqal. Raw, uncut Raqal is dull until it touches blood. It then absorbs a portion of what touches it and glows ruby, deeper the stronger the blood. A human barely tints it, a Vatrajaxo shifts it further, and a Vatrajaxu saturates it to a near-blinding depth. Anyone who knows the stone can read a wearer’s vitality from how the ring glows.

Raw deposits lie in and beneath boulders along the boundary between Pharuhona and TeDrona, an uncomfortable stretch to mine, which keeps the raw stone rare however common finished rings appear at court. When a ring is given, in the ceremony of recognition that means you are one of us, the recipient’s blood is pressed into the raw stone before final setting. That keys the glow to them. A bonded ring cannot be worn meaningfully by anyone else, so a forged or stolen inheritance claim is detectable. Sadok, Lioshe, all eight Lamqa, the Jamija and any future prince or princess wear one.

A folk rumour says a Raqelath is forged from a piece of someone’s heart-stone. It is not quite right, but it is not nothing either.

Shaelith, the circlet

Named for Shaelruq and Shaelrukan. The ring marks family, and the circlet marks office. It is worn only by those who hold one of Ishphaeno’s great governing seats. Both are polished sovoq, the same steel that arms the kingdom. No one wears a heavy European crown, because the circlet is designed around the wearer’s anatomy. Circlets and footwear are never counted in a wearer’s pleat rank.
