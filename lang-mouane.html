Mouane

A cipher daughter of French, with a Duala substrate, made by Black and Indigenous witch communities in the French colonial Americas.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Kanna reference documents. Tags mark anything less settled.

What it is

Mouane was born in the French colonial Louisiana and Caribbean of the 18th and 19th centuries. The Black and Indigenous witch communities there needed a shared tongue that was not French, not Bantu and not any single heritage, something forged in the space where all of them met. Decided

Its skeleton is French sound and spelling. Its substrate is Bantu, mainly Duala, a language of coastal Cameroon with its own long history of contact with French. Its cipher is the community’s own act of creation: a fixed set of changes to French that you can only apply correctly if you already know French.

The name comes from the Duala word muna, “child” (plural bana), respelled through French convention as Mouane. It names what its speakers are: children of more than one world. Regular derivation would give Mouna, but the name stays Mouane by decree of the community. It is the only word treated this way. Decided

Two registers

RegisterAlso calledUsed forCharacter
Mouane FortFortFormal, written and academic speechThe full cipher and full French grammar. The prestige standard.
Mouane LégerLégerEveryday speech, film and musicSimpler tenses, Duala and French fused pronouns, no grammatical gender.

The two are mutually intelligible. A Fort speaker always understands Léger. A Léger speaker can follow Fort but may not produce it correctly without formal study. The relationship echoes standard Canadian French and its spoken vernacular: the academy prescribes Fort, and Léger is what lives in the community.

How the cipher works

Every content word is derived from French in six steps. Proper nouns and Bantu-origin words skip the sound changes and keep their spelling.

StepWhat happens
1Classify the word. Content words follow their sound. Proper nouns and Bantu words follow their spelling.
2Write down how it sounds: split any word with a tréma, resolve digraphs and vowel units, drop silent letters, apply the e caduc rule, and flag nasals.
3Mark the nasal syllables as anchors. They never move.
4Reverse the order of all the other syllables.
5Shift the vowel of the new final syllable one step along its chain.
6Write the result in French spelling.

The four vowel chains

ChainSequence
Maina, o, è, i, é, back to a
Back roundedou, oi, ui, œ, eu, back to ou
Front roundedu, ey, ay, euy, back to u
Y-glideyé, yèr, yèl, yeur, back to yé

Open vowels (è, ê, ô) do not shift. A word with one syllable is augmented before ciphering: Fort adds the ending -é and Léger adds the prefix ma-. A word that is only a nasal anchor is left alone. Verbs are ciphered in the infinitive and then take ordinary French endings. The irregular verbs être, avoir, aller, faire, vouloir, pouvoir and savoir are protected. Decided

Stress

The shifted vowel of the last syllable carries the main stress, marked with an acute accent, and the original French stress stays as a weaker second stress. When the shift lands on a glide chain result, the accent goes on the first letter of the pair (ói, óu, óe), except ui, which takes it on the i (uí). Bonjour becomes bonjóir and bonsoir becomes bonsuír. When the last syllable is a nasal anchor, the shift lands on the last free syllable and the accent goes on that vowel, as in talmán for tellement. Decided

Numbers

Mouane counts with a mixture. The numbers one to ten are Duala. Seventy, eighty and ninety are Duala tens, which removes the French soixante-dix, quatre-vingt and quatre-vingt-dix. The numbers 17 to 19 join the Duala ten dôm to a French unit. Everything else is French: 11 to 16, 20 to 69, and 100 and above. Léger leaves the French numbers plain, and Fort ciphers them. Decided

Real languages behind it

Verlan, the French argot that reverses syllables, is the main inspiration for the cipher. Michif, the Métis mixed language of French and Cree, is the closest real parallel. The loss of grammatical gender follows what happened in Haitian Creole and Louisiana Creole. Camfranglais, the street speech of Cameroon, and the code languages of the Gullah Geechee are further parallels. The Duala forms come from published Duala word lists and grammars.
