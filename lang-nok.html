Nọk

The language of the Sekh and the Anu, and the language the Nok faith calls the tongue of the gods.

Sounds and spelling

Several sounds are written as letter pairs. Each pair counts as one letter in the alphabet.

LettersSound
khVoiceless velar fricative, like the ch in Scottish loch
chVoiceless uvular fricative, a deeper sound at the back of the throat
nyPalatal nasal, like the ñ in Spanish
kwA k followed by a w
zhVoiced postalveolar fricative, like the s in measure
shVoiceless postalveolar fricative, as in English
ngVelar nasal, as in sing

The letter č (named čán) is a single letter, not a pair. It is the voiceless postalveolar affricate, the English ch in church. Do not confuse it with ch, the uvular sound above. In careful and formal speech the Standard and Northern dialects keep the uvular ch, and casual, tired or sick speech softens it to kh. The Western dialect says kh and the Southern says h, and both respell ch to match.

Nọk has ten vowels and four diphthongs: ai, ei, oi and au. Nasal vowels are written with an umlaut. An acute or grave accent changes the quality of a vowel, and a dot under a vowel changes its quality too.

Dialects

Nọk has one standard form and three regional dialects. Each dialect adds a few letters or blends of its own.

DialectNọk nameWhere and whenWhat it sounds likeExtra letters and blends
StandardZaukumùFormalized and taught from 500 CE in Sekh-funded schools and Zingan seminaries.A textbook form, like standard Mandarin. Educated speakers can all use it, and uneducated speakers can roughly follow it.None beyond the base alphabet
NorthernTaucùmùNorth America. Documented from 1869, though speakers were there earlier. The youngest dialect and the emerging common tongue of Nọk speakers there.Anglicisms, the letter c for /k/, slower drawled syllables, and more abbreviated speech.nd, br, st, nk, and c at the start of a word
WesternNasibumùThe coastal cities of Thímùjạnùrèt, from modern Senegal to Angola.More glottal stops, many d and t sounds corrupted to z, and a clear l at the start of a syllable.mgb, l and bh
SouthernRanakumùngSouth America, especially Brazil. Large numbers of Nọk speakers arrived in the 1300s.Vowels and intonation colored by Portuguese, and a nasal ng that punctuates sentences.ts, tsh, nd and hw

Shared blends in every dialect are kw, gw, gb, ngw, nw, mw and mb.

Understanding between dialects

Standard Nọk is the bridge. Every dialect follows it easily, and uneducated speakers still roughly understand it. Taucùmù and Zaukumù are almost fully mutual: the differences are the r sound, c for /k/ at the start of a word, anglicisms and slower speech. Ranakumùng and Zaukumù are highly intelligible. A reduced final vowel, th said as t or s, and Portuguese-coloured intonation slow a listener down, but the words stay recognisable. Nasibumù is the hardest for the others. Its shifts are regular but heavy (d to z, t to l or d, p to b, k to g before close vowels) and it uses more glottal stops, so casual Nasibumù is hard to follow on first hearing. A few days of exposure fixes that, because the shifts have no exceptions. Nasibumù and Ranakumùng or Taucùmù in casual speech is the furthest pair, and speakers fall back on Standard to be sure.
