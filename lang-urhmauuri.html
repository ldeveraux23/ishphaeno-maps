Urhmauuri

The language of the deep-ocean Urhmaiia, with five politeness levels, a base-28 number system and sounds made for water.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Kanna reference documents. Tags mark anything less settled.

This page follows the Urhmauuri Language Guide of November 2026 and Canon Reference v.6. Dialects are on their own branch below.

Three ways of speaking

Underwater, telepathy is the main channel. It needs touch to reach non-telepaths, and some humans cannot receive it at all. Haulorai, the uvular call speech, carries over long distances for mourning calls, mate calls and soothing animals, and it alone keeps the ancient consonant-cluster onsets. Spoken Urhmauuri uses the voice. Speaking aloud with breath is intimate, private or formal, and dry rooms are where the Respectful and Royal endings are most likely to be said literally. Urhmauuka have a full larynx. Urhmate lack one and use cranial clicks, limb resonance and gill pulses instead. Decided

The language has two valid ways to write certain sounds: the formal spelling uses the real IPA symbol, and the keyboard spelling uses a simple stand-in. Neither is more correct. When typing in English, uu and ii are the proper romanizations of ʉ and ɨ, so naʉa is typed nauua. Some older words write the /j/ sound with the letter j, as in Spanish: Jorhdu, Jaiotsetsho and Aijena. It is the same sound as y.

Vowels

The front family is i, e and ii (ɨ). The back and central family is a, o, u and uu (ʉ). A short /ʊ/ appears only where u sits next to /ʁ/. Vowel harmony is a tendency, not a rule: a word rooted in a often stays in back vowels and a word rooted in i often stays in front vowels, but mixing is allowed in every register. The vowel ii sits in both families and often acts as a linker. Decided

Any two vowels may sit together, but two in a row make one syllable only if the second is i or u. That gives the single syllables ai, au, ei, eu, oi, ui and iu. Every other pair is two syllables: ia, io, ua, uo, oa, ao, oe and ae. The vowels uu and ii are special bridge vowels. A pair that starts with one and ends in a vowel (uua, uuo, iia, iio) is two syllables, while uui and iiu are one syllable. Canon breakdowns follow this: mai·tau·u·a, ru·a·la, so·a. Decided

Syllable and sound rules

  • No codas, with one exception. The only legal final consonant is the sacred rh, as in Urh, water. This does not license other finals.
  • No clusters in living speech. A consonant cluster at the start of a syllable survives only in the oldest sacred Haulorai, such as mourning calls.
  • An apostrophe marks hiatus when two vowels sit in separate syllables, as in vo’im.
  • The sound lh in native roots appears only at the start of a syllable, never at the end of a syllable or word. Seeing it signals deep oceanic heritage. In borrowed words it is allowed only next to a uvular or after a back vowel.
  • There is no w, v or q. The sound /w/ was retired and its glide survives inside au and ua. The sound /v/ was never admitted.

Borrowed or older words containing w, bh or v are respelled by their dominant vowel family: sh in front-vowel words, ny in back-vowel or unclear words, and q becomes k. So tuva becomes tunya, and the Urhmaiia animals once spelled with a v are now Nyohuraki, Kunyamuma and Kunyatsura. A zh is written z and an f is written h. Decided

Stress and pitch

Urhmauuri is syllable-timed, so each syllable gets roughly equal time. A two-syllable word is stressed on the first syllable. A word of three or more is stressed on the second-to-last, unless a suffix overrides it. Compounds keep the root’s stress, and hiatus is never collapsed. Pitch is not meaningful in daily speech, but it marks register. Decided

RegisterPitch
Informal, plainFlat or gently falling at the end of the sentence

Whistle vowels belong to Royal Polite and ceremonial declarations only. Urhmauuka whistle with the larynx, and Urhmate make comparable high tones by cranial clicks, limb resonance and gill pulses.

The five politeness levels

Politeness is marked once, on the final verb of the sentence, by dropping -ai and adding an ending. A speaker who opens in one level carries it through the sentence. Decided

LevelEndingUsed for
InformalnoneFriends, peers and equals
Plain polite-oStandard address in most formal or polite situations
Intimate-eA mate or life partner only (a Rinahiru or Maitauua)
Respectful-iSomeone of higher status or age
Royal-uThose who hold royal titles only

A stem that loses -ai takes the ending directly: maitai becomes maiti, and naikai becomes naiki. If the join is awkward an epenthetic n or an is inserted: masai becomes mas + an + i, masani. This system is separate from the six religious-name registers used for the creator. See Gods and faith.

Tense and aspect

Three helper verbs stay in fixed form: irainai (present anchor), iraimanai (past anchor) and iraishinai (progressive). Tense infixes sit before the conjugating syllable: -ma- for the past and -shi- for the future, with the longer forms -ima- and -eshi- repairing clusters. The model verb is masai, “to see.” Decided

The conditional and subjunctive endings are -elhai and -ezai, which lose -ai like the politeness endings do: anetezo, “if one can walk,” and anetelho, “then one can walk.” The relative pronoun is beiti, “who” or “that,” and it takes the same cases. Decided

The copula, the passive and evidence

The copula irai, “to be,” takes the politeness endings like any verb: iraino and iraini. As a helper in the tenses above it stays in its fixed form. The passive has no new ending. The bare past verb works as a participle and the copula follows, and an expressed agent takes -re. Decided

VoiceSentenceMeaning
ActiveNaʉa esana mamasoI saw the fish.
PassiveNaʉare esa mamaso irainoThe fish was seen by me.
Present passiveEsa masai irainoThe fish is seen.

Evidentials are optional and used for emphasis or to contradict someone else’s account. A marker goes after the politeness ending. Unmarked speech is the neutral default, and it covers everything else: shared Maitauzelai memory, Haulorai songs and reports from across the ocean. Such knowledge is real at some remove, never assumed false. Decided

The accusative and genitive are confirmed on nouns and pronouns. The dative is confirmed on a pronoun. The locative and ablative are so far attested only on the demonstratives. There is also an emphasis particle -ke after a case ending (naʉaka-ke, “in me, not someone else”) and a suffix -kao for motion toward a goal (leruka, “on the shore,” and lerukao, “toward the shore”).

Demonstratives

There are three degrees of distance. Proximal means in my immediate space, medial means between us in shared, reachable space, and distal means far from both or out of reach. Decided

DistanceSingularPlural
Proximal, thissoasoami
Medial, thatroaroami
Distal, that far awaytoatoami

Standing alone, a demonstrative takes case endings: soana, soada, soara and so on. With a noun that already carries a case ending it stays in the base form, so soa esana is “this fish” as an object. The medial dative roareha is irregular on purpose, to avoid a murmured run of vowels and because -rha already has another meaning. The word soa is also the root “walk, creep” (Kirusoa). The two never fill the same slot, so the clash is allowed.

Directions

The Urhmaiia use no left, right, front or back. All space is anchored to fixed axes, the way Guugu Yimithirr speakers in Australia use compass directions for everything, even body parts (source: Stephen Levinson, Space in Language and Cognition, 2003). It fits the biology, because every Urhmaiia has a passive geomagnetic compass sense, and a child learns the directions before numbers. Decided

Two systems coexist, and which one is primary marks class and origin. In the deep Maubuʁ interior there is no shore and the Toritunua currents are steady, so the poles and the vertical axis come first. In fringe cities the shore and currents are local and meaningful, so those come first. An elite Maubuʁ Urhmauuka using the poles in a fringe city sounds oddly formal, and a fringe Urhmate entering Maubuʁ must switch to the poles, a disorientation that marks class.

A direction is a root plus -oni after a consonant or -lhoni after a vowel. Body parts use the same axes, so a left arm and a right arm become the pole-arm and the equator-arm. The roots zoru (storm, pressure) and mai (love) sit in the pole directions on purpose, so that mai- reads as a pull toward the pole.

The question words share the root sei- and are exempt from vowel harmony. Naha means “no” and “not.” It stands before the verb (Naha masani!, “Don’t look!”) and also serves as the minus sign and the not-equals sign in numbers. Decided

Word building

  • Compounding joins two roots: Naua + lorai becomes Naualorai, “shell song.”
  • Elision trims a real word at the seam to avoid a pile of vowels: izilo + oaunaia becomes Iziloaunaia.
  • The agent ending -ii makes “person associated with”: Ezil becomes Ezilii, and zoru becomes Zoruii, a vent-brewer.
  • The language ending -ri is the suffix form of uuri: gizo + ri becomes gizori, the human language.
  • The ending -na turns an operator into a noun: orha becomes orhana.
  • Time-unit plurals take -sha: Orhdusha, Kirhdusha.
  • Reduplication appears once, in Haulohaura, “call upon call,” and is approved for poetry and call speech.

Two near-synonyms can split by scope: Lera is the earth as a planet, a force or nature, while zekha is the earth as dirt and ground underfoot. Decided

Numbers follow the verb-final order, with the operands first and the operator last. Grouped parts replace brackets by turning the operator into a noun. So 5 + 2 + 3 = 10 is boa bi iru orha obanua irai, and (10 + 3) × 2 is oba iru orhana bi shirina irai. In word problems irai conjugates: imarai (past), ishirai (future), and iro, ire, iri and iru for the four marked levels. The Royal iru is a deliberate homophone of the numeral 3, and context decides which is meant.
