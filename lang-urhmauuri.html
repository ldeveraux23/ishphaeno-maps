Urhmauuri

The language of the deep-ocean Urhmaiia, with five politeness levels, a base-28 number system and sounds made for water.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Kanna reference documents. Tags mark anything less settled.

This page follows the Urhmauuri Language Guide v2 (November 2026, with the rulings made up to 16 November) and Canon Reference v.6. Dialects are on their own branch below.

Three ways of speaking

Underwater, telepathy is the main channel. It needs touch to reach non-telepaths, and some humans cannot receive it at all. Haulorai, the uvular call speech, carries over long distances for mourning calls, mate calls and soothing animals. Its sounds include clicks and whistles that ordinary speech lacks, and it alone keeps the ancient consonant-cluster onsets. Spoken Urhmauuri uses the voice. Speaking aloud with breath is intimate, private or formal, and dry rooms are where the Respectful and Royal endings are most likely to be said literally. Decided

Urhmauuka have a full larynx. Urhmake keep their larynx and add vibration sacs. Whether an Urhmate has a larynx varies by lineage, and those without one use cranial clicks, limb resonance and gill pulses instead. Decided

The language has two valid ways to write certain sounds: the formal spelling uses the real IPA symbol, and the keyboard spelling uses a simple stand-in. Neither is more correct. When typing in English, uu and ii are the proper romanizations of ʉ and ɨ, so naʉa is typed nauua. Decided

The j and y rule. Some existing words write the /j/ sound with the letter j, as in Spanish: Jorhdu, Jaiotsetsho, Aijena and Ejasantua. Those spellings are frozen as legacy forms. Every new word writes /j/ with y. Decided

Vowels

The front family is i, e and ii (ɨ). The back and central family is a, o, u and uu (ʉ). A short /ʊ/ appears only where u sits next to /ʁ/. Vowel harmony is a tendency, not a rule: a word rooted in a often stays in back vowels and a word rooted in i often stays in front vowels, but mixing is allowed in every register. The vowel ii sits in both families and often acts as a linker. Decided

Any two vowels may sit together, but two in a row make one syllable only if the second is i or u. That gives the single syllables ai, au, ei, eu, oi, ui and iu. Every other pair is two syllables: ia, io, ua, uo, oa, ao, oe and ae. The vowels uu and ii are special bridge vowels. A pair that starts with one and ends in a vowel (uua, uuo, iia, iio) is two syllables, while uui and iiu are one syllable, like any pair that ends in i or u. Canon breakdowns follow this: mai·tau·u·a, ru·a·la, so·a. A few older words, such as ho·ze·i·ra, still split ei. Decided

Syllable and sound rules

  • No codas, with one exception. The only legal final consonant is the sacred rh, as in Urh, water. This does not license other finals.
  • No clusters in living speech. A consonant cluster at the start of a syllable survives only in the oldest sacred Haulorai, such as mourning calls.
  • An apostrophe marks hiatus when two vowels sit in separate syllables, as in vo’im.
  • The sound lh in native roots appears only at the start of a syllable, never at the end of a syllable or word. Seeing it signals deep oceanic heritage. In borrowed words it is allowed only next to a uvular or after a back vowel (u, o, uu, a).
  • There is no w, v or q. The sound /w/ was retired and its glide survives inside au and ua. The sound /v/ was never admitted.
  • Labials help underwater. New high-frequency grammar favours labials (m, b, p) and rounded or open vowels, because lip closure is easy to read underwater. Decided

Adapting old words and loanwords

Words containing w, bh or v are respelled by their dominant vowel family. A q becomes k, zh becomes z and f becomes h. The animals once spelled with a v are now Nyohuraki, Kunyamuma and Kunyatsura, and Zheuma is Zeuma. Decided

Stress and pitch

Urhmauuri is syllable-timed, so each syllable gets roughly equal time. A two-syllable word is stressed on the first syllable. A word of three or more is stressed on the second-to-last, unless a suffix overrides it. Compounds and affixed forms keep the root’s stress, with an optional secondary stress on a suffix. Hiatus is never collapsed: each vowel is its own syllable and is said distinctly. Pitch is not meaningful in daily speech, but it marks register, ritual and emotion. Decided

Whistle vowels belong to Royal Polite and ceremonial declarations only. They sit on the main vowel of the root and on the final verb syllables, and the romanization marks them with acute accents. Urhmauuka whistle with the larynx, and Urhmate make comparable high tones by cranial clicks, limb resonance and gill pulses. Haulorai chant mode is the sung register for rituals, sacred poetry and invocations. Its older name in the early stress notes was Egoria.

Verbs

Urhmauuri is verb-final. Every verb has an infinitive that ends in -ai. Tense, mood and politeness are all built from the stem, which is the infinitive without -ai. Tense infixes go inside the stem, mood endings go after it, and a politeness ending replaces -ai at the very end. A stem always ends in a consonant, so every ending can attach to it directly. The five politeness levels

Politeness is marked once, on the final verb of the sentence, by dropping -ai and adding an ending. A speaker who opens in one level carries it through the sentence. The informal register keeps the infinitive as it is, including -ai. Decided

Informalnone (keeps -ai)Friends, peers and equals
Plain polite-oStandard address in most formal or polite situations
Intimate-eA mate or life partner only (a Rinahiru or Maitauua)
Respectful-iSomeone of higher status or age
Royal-uThose who hold royal titles only

The ending joins the stem directly: maitai becomes mait + i, maiti, and naikai becomes naiki. If the join is awkward an epenthetic n or an is inserted before the ending: masai becomes mas + an + i, masani. This system is separate from the six religious-name registers used for the creator. See Gods and faith. Decided

Commands

The imperative is the bare infinitive, and it is blunt: herai! “Help!” Adding a politeness ending softens it: heri! “Please help!” An exclamation mark shows emotional intensity and does not by itself make a command. Decided

Tense and aspect

Mood

The subjunctive is -ezai and the conditional is -elhai. They go after the stem and after any tense infix, and then the politeness ending replaces -ai: anetai becomes anetezo, “if one can walk,” and anetelho, “then one can walk.” The past subjunctive of masai is mamasezo. A mood verb inside a longer sentence keeps its full -ai form until it is the last one. Decided

The copula, the passive and evidence

The copula irai, “to be,” takes the politeness endings like any verb: iraino and iraini. When it is the final verb of a sentence it carries the ending. As a helper in the tenses above it stays in its fixed form. Decided

The passive has no new ending. The past verb works as the participle in its full -ai form, because only the final verb takes a politeness ending, and the copula follows and carries it. An expressed agent takes -re. Decided

Unmarked speech is the neutral default, and it covers everything else: shared Maitauzelai memory, Haulorai songs and reports from across the ocean. The electromagnetic Ruala

Every Urhmaiia has a passive geomagnetic compass. Some Urhmauuka develop an enhanced electromagnetic Ruala on top of it. As with all Ruala, a person gets one type only. Ordinary Urhmaiia know where they are. This type knows where things have been. The language keeps a marker for it, the evidential -rhua above. The three skills are on Castes and bodies. Decided

Tools and causing

Nouns, cases and phrases

Nouns do not mark plural, and context shows number. Case endings go on the noun, and everything else in the phrase stays bare. The default clause order is subject, object, verb. Object, subject, verb is allowed when the object is the topic or is being stressed. Decided

All of these endings are confirmed on ordinary nouns as well as on pronouns and demonstratives. A noun that ends in -rh (such as honoarh) takes the endings directly. An optional emphasis particle -ke follows a case ending: naʉaka-ke, “in me, not someone else.” Two different -na endings sound alike: the accusative, and the nominalizer that turns an operator or a clause into a noun. When both apply they stack as -nana. Decided

Noun phrase order

The order inside a noun phrase is demonstrative, possessor with -da, noun, adjective. The case ending goes on the noun only, and the adjective stays bare. Examples: soa esada notori, “this fish’s fin,” and, built from the rules, Naʉa soa tiada esana mamaso, “I saw this fish of yours.” Adverbs stand just before the verb. Time and place settings stand at the start of the sentence. Decided

Standing alone, a demonstrative takes case endings. With a noun that already carries a case ending it stays in the base form, so soa esana is “this fish” as an object, while soana alone means “this” as an object and soada notori means “this one’s fin.” The medial dative roareha is irregular on purpose, to avoid a murmured run of vowels and because -rha already has another meaning. The word soa is also the root “walk, creep” (Kirusoa). The two never fill the same slot, so the clash is allowed. A demonstrative and a direction stay separate words: soa haulhoni, “this thing, deep-ward.” Decided

Directions

The Urhmaiia use no left, right, front or back. All space is anchored to fixed axes, the way Guugu Yimithirr speakers in Australia use compass directions for everything, even body parts (source: Stephen Levinson, Space in Language and Cognition, 2003). It fits the biology, because every Urhmaiia has a passive geomagnetic compass sense, and a child learns the directions before numbers and cannot be disoriented the way a human can. Decided

Two systems coexist, and which one is primary marks class and origin. In the deep Maubuʁ interior there is no shore and the Toritunua currents are steady, so the poles and the vertical axis come first. In fringe cities the shore and currents are local and meaningful, so those come first, with the poles always available but used less. An elite Maubuʁ Urhmauuka using the poles in a fringe city sounds oddly formal, and a fringe Urhmate entering Maubuʁ must switch to the poles, a disorientation that marks class.

Body parts use the same axes, so a left arm and a right arm become the pole-arm and the equator-arm. The question words share the root sei- and are exempt from vowel harmony. A question word stays in the place where the answer would stand and takes the same case ending as the answer would. “What did you see?” puts seilana where esana, “the fish” as an object, would go, so the answer repeats the question’s shape. The sei- words never take nei. Decided

For a yes or no question, add nei at the very end of the sentence, after the politeness ending and any evidential: Maso nei?, “Do you see?” The answers are la (yes) and naha (no). Naha also means “not.” It stands before the verb (Naha masani!, “Don’t look!”) and serves as the minus sign and the not-equals sign in numbers. Decided

There is no general place suffix. Place words are separate roots: tira (garden, growing place), tunua (city) and tori (road, current road). A place-name compound is just two roots joined, so Toritunua means “current road of cities.” Decided

Two stone roots. They look alike but are not interchangeable. Kohu is stone as a hard material or quality, as in Borukohu and Kohuhaulo (stone horn). Roka is rock as a natural mass or landform, as in Rokaurhona. Near-synonyms can also split by scope: Lera is the earth as a planet, a force or nature, while zekha is the earth as dirt and ground underfoot. Decided

Compounding joins two roots: Naua + lorai becomes Naualorai, “shell song.” Elision trims a real word at the seam to avoid a pile of vowels: izilo + oaunaia becomes Iziloaunaia. Time words use it too: soa + aldisha becomes soldisha (today), and aldisha + torilhoni becomes aldorilhoni (tomorrow), where the t is voiced to d. Bound roots are citation-only fragments that never stand alone, so the no-coda rule does not apply to them. Reduplication appears in Haulohaura and is approved for poetry, call speech, intensives and the large-number word baulabaula. Decided

Plural. Nouns do not mark plural. Time units are the one exception: they take -sha every time (Orhdusha, Jorhdusha, Kirhdusha). Aldisha, Alesha and Ilesha are fixed words and not plurals. Decided

Roots from real languages

Urhmauuri takes some roots from the African, Southeast Asian and Pacific languages of the first two waves of Urhmaiia. The first is ajarai, “to teach,” from Malay ajar with the verb ending -ai, which ties it to the first wave and the Sundaland coast. From it come Ajarii, “teacher,” and Urhonaiajarii, “the scout teachers.” More roots will be worked in over time, each adapted to the sound rules. Decided

Maulaza is the vast cycle, and that many days is about 60 Earth years. A span between two ages is a subtraction, with the larger number first because the first operand is always the one subtracted from. The result takes -nua. So 30 − 13 = 17 is laza-bi nuba nura rubinua irai, and 150 − 60 = 90 is boalaza-oba bilaza-ao nura irulaza-beinua irai. Adding spans uses orha in the same way. A span stated on its own is the number with aleshada, so “17 years” is rubi aleshada. “From 13 to 30” can use the ablative and the goal ending: nubaze laza-bikao. Proposed

Round numbers

Urhmauuri has no separate names for round numbers. The round numbers in base 28 are the multiples of laza (28), baula (784) and maulaza (21,952), and they are written out like any other number: 56 is bilaza, 392 is ibialaza and 1,568 is bibaula. Two things make them easier to say. Decided

Building a sentence

  1. Order the words subject, object, verb. The verb comes last. Put any time or place setting at the start.
  2. Build the noun phrase as demonstrative, possessor with -da, noun, adjective, with the case ending on the noun only.
  3. Mark the nouns. Use -na for the object, -da for “of” and “my” or “your,” -re for “by,” -ra for “to,” -ka for “at,” -kao for “toward” and -ze for “from.”
  4. Put adverbs just before the verb.
  5. Build the verb from its stem: add -ma- (past) or -shi- (future) before the stem’s final consonant, then -ezai or -elhai for mood if needed.
  6. Pick the register from the listener: informal (keeps -ai), plain polite (-o), intimate (-e), respectful (-i) or royal (-u). Mark it only on the final verb.
  7. Add an evidential only if needed. Say -mua when stressing personal experience, and put it after the politeness ending.
  8. Negate with naha before the verb.
  9. Make it a question by adding nei at the end for yes or no, or by putting a sei- word where the answer would go. Never use both.
  10. Join clauses with me, au or aka, or with a nominalized verb plus -ka, -ze, -ra or -re.
  11. Check the writing. Urhtsorna has no letters for w, v, q, f or zh, so words are respelled. New words write /j/ with y.

Coining a word

  1. Allowed sounds only? Check the consonant table. No w, v or q.
  2. Legal syllables? Every syllable must be CV, VCV, CVV or V. A final consonant other than -rh is not legal.
  3. Can a prefix or suffix do it? Check the word-building table first.
  4. Bound root or whole word? A citation-only fragment never surfaces alone, so its ending does not matter.
  5. Compounding two real words? If the join makes a double vowel or clash, elide or add a linking vowel.
  6. Already claimed? Search the root list. If you want a near-synonym on purpose, make the difference clear, as with Lera and zekha, or kohu and roka.
  7. Old or borrowed word? Respell w, bh and v as sh (front vowels) or ny (back vowels), q as k, zh as z and f as h.
  8. Mind the lh rule. In native roots it starts a syllable only.
  9. Write /j/ as y in every new word.
