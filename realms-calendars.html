Earth

Almost everyone on Earth uses the ordinary Gregorian calendar. The Nok keep their own, built to map onto it. It has twelve named months of thirty days followed by a five-day sacred month, six days in a Gregorian leap year. The week has six days, and the year opens in February. The epoch is 379 BCE. The full tables, the festival year and the name-day tradition are on Calendar and festivals.

The Nok word for the calendar is gbasídíma or gbasíméjídíma, “record of days.” The Name Calendar is Khínai’gbasídí. Decided

Ishphaeno

Ishphaeno has no sun and no moon, so its day is kept by a living clock and its year by the sky. Time also runs slower there than on Earth. One Ishphaeno hour is six Earth hours.

The Xathaeli

The Xathaeli is a native organism that looks like an anemone, kept in an open ceramic or stone pot in homes, shops, government buildings and ships. It cycles through four colours, one for each quarter of the day, and its pulse tells you how far through the quarter you are. Read it from the corner of your eye, the way you notice a ceiling fan slowing down. See the Xathaeli for the animal itself.

Each colour lasts two periods, which is 200 minutes. The first half is still. The second half pulses, slowly at first, then faster and faster, until a long, slow bloom of the new colour arrives, like an exhale.

Wild Xathaeli are erratic and make poor timekeepers, so households keep cultivated ones, bred for a steady pulse and brighter colour. A dying or irregular Xathaeli is deeply disorienting. Households whose Xathaeli dies during Qoruvrak, when replacements are hard to find, feel real anxiety.

The pot is watered and sprinkled with ash twice a week.

The tables count 100 minutes to a period, so the period is also an hour of 100 minutes. Proposed

The six aurora seasons

The sky is lit by the Toluqavel, Toluqavadoth’s inner state made visible. Its colour sets the season. There is a most-common order, followed about 85 per cent of the time, but Toluqavadoth is Primordial Chaos and the order shifts. The lengths vary within the ranges below. Almanac scholars and diviners make probabilistic predictions, and none can ever be fully accurate. See Toluqavadoth and the sky map.

2Sulfur yellow-goldVeluphe / Pheluvorath11 to 13 weeksTrade, harvest and abundance. The sobi mines are busiest and the black market peaks. The sky turns the colour of money.

6Cobalt blueQoruvrak / Qoruvrath2 to 5 weeksThe liminal disaster season: earthquakes, eruptions, tidal surges and higher chaos.

The six ranges add up to a year of 49 to 62 weeks. The shift from season five to season six is deliberate, and the cosmology has a reason for it. The most feared event is Qoruvrak twice in close succession. It has happened at least once, and it became a legendary catastrophic era.

Each season has one colour, and its shade varies subtly through the season. The Toluqavel crosses the sky like a very wide sun, on a daily rhythm that wanders, so it is not reliable enough to tell time. Decided

The tides

The Xatheloru tides are Toluqavadoth’s breathing. They are used for coastal navigation, reading its mood and omens, and never for telling time. A suddenly irregular tide means something is disturbing its rest, and in Qoruvrak it breathes harder, with storm surges and flooding.

Aempaera

Aempaera has no night. Torusheva, the luminous orb Jangshe made, hangs above the realm and never sets. Over a long cycle it slowly bleaches from full brightness to a dim moonlight, then brightens again. When it is dim, the stars come out, the same stars seen from Earth from another angle. See Aempaera and Jangshe.

The Shorumael and the wind

Because the orb never moves across the sky, shadows always point the same way, so a normal sundial is useless. What changes is shadow length. The Shorumael is a vertical post or obelisk with rings marked at measured distances on the ground around it. A bright orb gives a short shadow and a dim orb a long one, so the shadow on the rings gives the broad period.

Wind gives the finer reading. Light, frequent gusts mean the morning equivalent, strong longer gusts the afternoon, and gentler medium gusts the evening. Brightness and wind together give complete timekeeping. A Vatrajarə who is blind or has impaired senses can tell the time by wind alone.

Day and week

  • Torusheva’s brightening and dimming is the nearest thing Aempaera has to day and night. One full cycle, from full brightness through moonlight and back, is one day. Decided
  • A day is 24 Aempaera hours. Because an Earth hour holds six Aempaera hours, a day lasts 4 Earth hours. Decided
  • The week is six days, which is exactly one Earth day. Decided
  • The two seasons are counted in weeks. Hevatoru has more moonlit days than Romatoru. Decided

Kanna

Kanna circles its orange star Hvan once in 211.45 Earth days, which is 192 local days of 26.43 hours each. Its year is 0.579 of an Earth year, and the 26-degree axial tilt turns the seasons about 1.7 times faster than Earth’s. See The Hvan system.

Before the Calamity

The old calendar belonged to the twin moons. A year was exactly 192 local days, with no drift.

UnitLengthSet by
Month16 local daysOne full Kiruvyn phase cycle, exactly
Year12 months, 192 local daysTwelve Kiruvyn cycles
Week4 local daysFour to a month, twelve to a season, forty-eight to a year
Season48 local days, four to a yearThe solar year
The wandering calendar34.9 local daysKiravin’s cycle, which deliberately does not divide the year

Kiruvyn, “Eye of the Ebb,” keeps a nearly circular orbit and never changes in the sky, so it is the fixed calendar moon. Kiravin, “Eye of the Flow,” moves in an elliptical orbit and visibly swells and shrinks, so its cycle drifts against the year and produces a wandering ritual calendar beside the fixed solar one. In Nok the moons are Khùzú and Khùzí.

After the Calamity

The moons collided and merged into one, Sidlosa, also called Khùm. The new sky holds 8.886 moon-months in a year, which is not a whole number. Every descendant culture still keeps the twelve-month year as its religious calendar, out of reverence for a sky none of them has seen, so they count twelve months while the moon above them keeps a different rhythm. Civil life uses a solar calendar of eight months of 24 days with a six-day week, thirty-two weeks to the year, which the broken moons cannot disturb. The tides also changed, from an irregular two-moon beat to a steady one. See the Calamity.

Urhmaiia

The short day

The last day of every month is shortened so the calendar stays true to the moon. The short day at the end of the twelfth month lasts about 24¾ hours, almost exactly one surface day. It is the one day when Urhmaiia time and surface time nearly match, which is why the yearly watch happens on it. Decided

The watch of Antares

Twelve moon months fall about eleven days short of the solar year. To keep the seasons in place, every year on the short day of the twelfth month a scout goes up to the surface before dawn to look for Antares, the red star in the south, on the first dawns it can be seen after being lost in the sun’s glare. The Urhmaiia have their own name for it, meaning a parent’s love. Decided

  • The scribes. Keepers of the 19-year table predict each year’s leap month well ahead, seal the prediction in front of witnesses at the start of the short day, and open it after the sighting. If the scout and the scribes disagree, the sighting wins, and the quarrel becomes an omen or a scandal. Decided
  • The scout. Certain families train for the honour and nominate candidates. The priests choose one each year. In custom only an Urhmauuka is chosen, because they are fully amphibious. An Urhmate could technically do it. The scout may come ashore to watch if they wish, but the sighting can be made from the water’s surface. Decided
  • The ascent. From the deepest cities about one and a half to two hours, with a pause near the surface and a slow rise at the end. The whole trip is about seven hours. Proposed
  • The watch. The scout surfaces in the dark and treads water, facing the south-east corner of the sky. Antares rises about 45 minutes before sunrise. Its return to the dawn sky falls between about 23 and 28 December by the surface calendar. Decided
  • Star clear and bright. The year opens on time. Decided
  • Star hazy, pale or deep red. Each is read as an omen for the season. Decided
  • Sky clear and no star. The year is not finished, and a thirteenth month is added. A leap month is a full month of 20 Aldisha. Decided
  • Clouded out. The scout may try again at up to three more dawns, treading water, with those days counted outside the year as sacred days. Decided
  • Over 19 years. The leap months average seven in 19 years, and the new year slides within about a month after the star first appears. Proposed

Converting between realms

Earth is the neutral baseline for every conversion. Ishphaeno time runs slower and Aempaera time runs faster, which gives these rates.

ConversionRate
1 Ishphaeno hour6 Earth hours, or 36 Aempaera hours
1 Earth hour6 Aempaera hours
1 Aempaera hour10 Earth minutes (worked out from the rate above)
1 Ishphaeno day8 periods, 48 Earth hours
1 Ishphaeno week8 Ishphaeno days, 16 Earth days

Earth sits between Aempaera and Ishphaeno in how time flows. Guards of the punishment facility Zashraaxomavel serve their rotations measured in Earth years, because both sides need a common reference. One standard rotation is 60 Earth years, a full human lifetime on purpose. An Ishphaeno year is 2.1 to 2.7 Earth years, so that is about 22 to 28 Ishphaeno years, or about 360 Aempaera years at six to one.
