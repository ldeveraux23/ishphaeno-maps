Glossary and names

The terms, titles and naming rules of the universe in one place, with a link to the page that explains each.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the reference documents for each realm. Tags mark anything less settled. Numbers worked out from canon are marked as such.

How to read the names

The universe has many languages, and a name usually tells you which one it comes from. These are the patterns worth knowing.

Nok spellings use tone marks and dotted vowels. They follow the current reference documents, and the same name is always spelled the same way. Egyptian-looking words in the older documents are ignored unless they name a city or a place. Decided

Stamfaen and its daughters

  • Stamfaen always keeps its full ae. The daughter languages reduce it to ê.
  • è is a stressed ɛ.
  • The names of peoples carry the ê: Lyndfê̂, Rejfê, Kannafê, Sharafê. The Vorfae name stays unreduced.
  • The Dorrwfø and the Mylzhfã call themselves by those names, with a plain-text spelling for typing.

The full family tree is on Stamfaen family.

The O- and Te- in the Fall’s names are each language’s definite article. The old spelling Oshalani is retired. Decided

Both languages allow two spellings of certain sounds. The formal spelling uses the real IPA symbol, such as ə, ħ, ʕ or ʁ. The working spelling uses a plain keyboard stand-in. They are the same sound in different settings, and neither is more correct. The almanac uses the formal spelling.

Urhmauuri

  • Every syllable is one of four shapes: a consonant and a vowel, a vowel, a consonant and two vowels, or a vowel, a consonant and a vowel. Words almost never end in a consonant. The exception is -rh.
  • There is no w, v or q. Old words that had them were changed to sh or ny, depending on their vowels.
  • kh is one sound, not two. The same is true of rh, which has a formal spelling with ʁ.
  • The ending -ii turns a concept into a person: Ezil plus -ii is Ezilii.
  • An apostrophe marks two vowels that are not blended, as in Vo’im, which is two syllables.
  • Two names exist for the same people. Urhmauua is what they call themselves, and Urhmaiia is what outsiders call them. The almanac uses Urhmaiia.

Urhmauuri speech has five politeness levels. They are marked on the final verb, and the almanac names them here.

Separate from these, the names of the gods have six registers of their own, from the formal high canon form down to the casual vernacular. Do not confuse the two systems. See the language page.
