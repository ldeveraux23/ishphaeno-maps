The float clock

A tower that sings the hour into the water, and the two crews who keep it in step with a sun nobody below can see.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Urhmaiia Canon Reference. Tags mark anything less settled. Terms are given in the keyboard romanisation, with the uvular letter written rh.

What it is

A float clock is the sound-keeping city clock that stands in the water. The tower crew turns it over and sings the hour in Haulorai, the way bells or calls to prayer carry the hour on land. Listeners read it on a cuff, a tuned reed that receives the call. The hour is told in the number of phrases sung. The tower is the Orhdutira and its crew is the Orhdutirii. Decided

The time it keeps is the native ocean time, the Aldisha, Orhdu, Jorhdu and Kirhdu of Calendars. Each Aldisha is divided into four watches of six Orhdu, one in the day and three in the night, and each watch is an Orhdulekha. Decided

Watch it run

A tall tube of glass sponge holds fluid, and a float rises through it at a steady speed. When it reaches the top, the crew flips the tube, the float starts again from the bottom, and the hour is called. One run is one Aldisha of 24 Orhdu. Thicker fluid slows the float and more lift speeds it, so the keepers tune the two against each other until a run takes exactly 24 Orhdu. Native time is the main reading. The surface hours and the sun clock are only outside references, and the two clocks line up again every two Aldisha. The sounds

Everything is tuned to E minor natural. The listening range is kept low, from about G2 to D4, so that the keynote and the city motifs sit in the deep register. The choices were made by ear. Decided

The tower crew

The crew is mixed, and each member uses the body they have. Urhmake keep the larynx and add vibration sacs. The sacs hold the keynote, the larynx sings the phrases, and Urhmate sound the percussive watch marker. The keynote holder and the watch leader are always Urhmauuka, chosen by seniority. The other seats stay open to whoever has the capacity. What seniority means differs from city to city, as regional colour: time served in the tower, or age, or caste rank. Decided

Two crews for the sun

Two separate groups keep the clock in step with the sun, and they work together. The Soratsorii record it and the Soraesii call it. The names are kept apart on purpose. The Soratsorii write down what the sun does, and the Soraesii carry the news to the tower. Decided

What it is made of

  • The float tube is glass sponge, Kohuzauma, as one uniform material for every clock. Cities too deep for the tube to work use a second compound, the Pabitira, or the help of Ruala as a special case. Decided
  • The fluid is a blend of oil and a spermaceti-like wax, the Loretoma. The wax comes from a whale-like animal, the Haulouma, harvested wild from whale falls. There is no domestication yet. The harvest crew is mixed too. Decided
  • The glass guild, the Kohuzii, works the glass. The Kohulago, “fallen stone,” are tektite beads. The Kohutsorii are the stone artisans, the carvers of Urhtsorna Roka. Decided
  • The cuff, Esabe, is the tuned reed pager that receives the call. Decided

Cities carry the call on stone horns, and the deepest cities use acoustic mirrors cut in stone. Shallow cities also use conches as small personal horns. Each fringe region has its own range and a tracking crew of one or two, who count the days on a cord of mussel byssus thread knotted onto a notched stone, since bone would wear away under water.
