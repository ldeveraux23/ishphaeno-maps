Birth, change and death

Urhmaiia are born into a song, change when they meet the right person, and are given back to the sea as a reef.

Lines without a tag are settled canon from the Urhmaiia Canon Reference. Tags mark anything less settled. Terms are given in the keyboard romanisation, with the uvular letter written rh.

Birth

  • A parent expels the embryo through the throat. It grows in a community natal centre, a Raunutua, in a translucent sac hung from a central living anchor of coral or root.
  • Gestation is six to nine human months, following the seasons more than the calendar. The season of release sets the season of hatching.
  • The sac, Raunuvera, is tough and leathery like a shark egg case. A sinew line, Maualini, runs from its loop to the anchor, fixed with a barnacle-cement glue.
  • A one-way feeding port lets a parent pass Lualini, a nutrient fluid, into the sac. Caretakers called Raunutii can feed on a family’s behalf.
  • No navel forms. Only Urhmake and transformed Urhmate, who begin life in ordinary human birth, have one.
  • All Urhmaiia are born Ezilii: intersex, with an insertive organ, a receptive vulva and an anus. Wealthier families may raise embryos in private. The ideology calls this superior, and the biology suggests otherwise.

The hatching, Jaiotsetsho

The embryo’s glow changes pattern in the days before it emerges. Neighbours gather at the Raunutua and sing Haulorai, which physically helps guide the hatchling out. A child born into a whole city’s song has an easier time than one hatched in near silence. A community hatching is open to all. A standard household invites family and allies. An elite private hatching has only retainers, and a quieter song.

The nameless window

No name is given for two years. The child doubles in size repeatedly, grows full gills and makes first Haulorai sounds. The first hatching day marks survival and the second marks identity. At the end of the second year, if the child is thriving, the family gathers in private. The child announces their own name in Haulorai, which is their first deliberate voiced communication. The name is then told to the community.

Names are social signals. A name that describes a trait is Urhmate, a name that invokes lineage is Urhmauuka, and a name that reaches forward is Urhmake.

Haza, the change

  • Haza is a puberty triggered by meeting a possible life partner (Maitauua), not by age alone. The usual window is 13 to 30.
  • Late Haza, 30 to 60, is rare. After 60 it is dangerous because the body is too set, and an unchanged Urhmaiia nearing 60 is a community worry.
  • Symptoms are intense body heat, severe cramps, hormonal swings, and rapid growth in height and bulk. Scaled Urhmate molt faster, and some grow new fins.
  • Uhinemi is the general change. Uhinaudia is the narrower shift in organ roles, and it can happen many times in life. An Urhmaiia may move from Arhtsilii (insertive) back to Ezilii (neutral) or on to Izilii (receptive) on meeting a new partner.
  • Gender is biologically fluid across a lifetime. All three states are honoured, and Ezilii is sacred as the original birth state.
  • Deliberately avoiding Haza by avoiding any possible partner is considered deeply strange. If the interest is not returned, the drive fades in about two months (ilesha).
  • Cross-species attraction still triggers the change.

Bonding

  • Maitauzelai is the nose-press bond. Glands on each side of the nose release pheromones and dopamine. It is done only with a life partner (Maitauua) or a destined mate (Rinahiru) and is never casual.
  • The death of a partner can kill an Urhmaiia or bring a long, deep depression.
  • Banauketa is reproductive copulation. It can be with a partner or a close friend (Kide). The two acts are distinct: Maitauzelai is for love and Banauketa is mainly reproductive.
  • A human female and an Arhtsilii can mate, as can a human male and an Izilii, but conception is rare with high fetal mortality. Surviving children are Urhmake, and the trait can skip generations.

Urhmake and the ripening

Tsitsia is the Urhmake’s version of Haza. It starts after human puberty, at about 17 to 20, and is triggered by the body’s own clock together with water exposure. Gills, water tolerance, telepathy and the magnetic sense develop. Haza does not occur, so the organ-prioritisation process never fires. Reproductive biology locks at the moment of Tsitsia. A trans Urhmake already on hormones before Tsitsia completes with a biology that matches their current hormones. Tsitsia honours the lived body, not the birth assignment.

Goria is a siren-like Haulorai call that pulls Urhmake descendants to the water. It can speed Tsitsia, but done too early it risks incomplete gills and stunted Ruala. The ripening has real dangers: predation by sea animals, missing the window by being too far inland, or losing the watcher who is meant to guide the child.

Death

There is no universal good or bad death. The specific wrong is death by pollution or at human hands, because it blocks the Ruuanakha: a poisoned body grows no reef. The Ruua leaves the body at death and flows toward the deepest, cleanest current, building up near Maubuʁ.

  • Ruuanakha, the ideal death: the body sinks into the deep, and over decades becomes a living reef of tube worms, vent crabs and other life. It feeds the sea for up to a century.
  • Zorugoria is the death call. Family and local guards swim to the body. They witness it, take a small keepsake (Ruuanai) such as a scale or fin fragment, and leave the body to the deep. Leaving is trust.
  • Zhaʉelekha is community mourning, sung in Haulorai for a month for a respected elder or a few days for a hatchling. The dead are remembered at seasonal gatherings.
  • The Ruuatira is the memorial garden, usually beside the Raunutua so birth and death share a place. The keepsake is planted, and the dead feed living plants. It is the only permanent institution that is alive rather than built.
  • The Naizhaʉe is an unofficial role in fringe cities: someone known for carrying bodies to deep water.

Eating an Urhmaiia

Consuming another Urhmaiia is the deepest wrong. The mainstream belief is that the consumed Ruua is trapped inside the eater and cannot return to the current. A fringe counter-belief holds that eating an enemy takes their strength and gifts. Communities that follow Dagan, the god of crossing boundaries, find the second idea easier to imagine, which is part of why Dagan worship draws suspicion from Urhaha-orthodox circles.
